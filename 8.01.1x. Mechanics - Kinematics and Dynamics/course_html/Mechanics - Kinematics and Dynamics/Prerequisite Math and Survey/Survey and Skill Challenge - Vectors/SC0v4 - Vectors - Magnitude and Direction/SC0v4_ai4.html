The Core Concepts

Understanding Vectors: Magnitude & Direction

Unlike scalar quantities (like temperature, mass, or time) which only have a magnitude, a vector represents a physical quantity with both a magnitude (how much) and a direction (which way). This workspace breaks down these properties as explained in physics curriculum modules.

01

Vectors vs Scalars

A vector is represented visually as an arrow. The length of the arrow represents the magnitude, and the angle it points relative to a reference coordinate system represents its direction.

Scalar Examples
Mass (50 kg), Speed (10 m/s), Temp (30°C)
Vector Examples
Force (50 N East), Velocity (10 m/s North)
Vectors possess full dimensional spatial info.

03

Force vs. Velocity Direction

Many beginner physics students assume that the velocity vector ( ) of an object must always point in the same direction as the force vector ( ) being applied. This is a misconception!

Why can they point in different directions?
  • Mechanical Constraints: Wheels or rails restrict an object to move only in one direction. Any diagonal force gets corrected by track normal force.
  • Momentum/Inertia: A flying space shuttle moving forward that turns its thrusters sideways has velocity forward, but thrust force directed sideways.
This dynamic was highlighted in the towing-toy video demonstration.

Common Misconceptions Addressed:

❌ "Negative vector means negative value"

No. A negative sign in vector coordinates indicates direction only (e.g. going left or going down), not a negative physical amount. Magnitude is always an absolute positive value.

❌ "Adding vectors is simple arithmetic"

No. You cannot add vectors like simple scalars ( unless they point in exact same direction). Instead, vectors must be broken into and components and added independently (head-to-tail method).
