Critical Intuition Checkpoint

Deep mental model about coordinate changes over time

In a Cartesian system, your reference vectors $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ are locked static to the universe. Their derivative with respect to time is exactly zero:

$$\frac{d\hat{i}}{dt} = 0, \quad \frac{d\hat{j}}{dt} = 0$$

However, in a **Polar System**, the radial axis $\hat{r}$ and angular axis $\hat{\theta}$ lock onto the moving particle! As the particle completes its sweep, the vectors tilt, warp, and spin. This is why:

$$\frac{d\hat{r}}{dt} = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\hat{\theta} = \omega\hat{\theta}$$
Step 1 Position Representation

Defining Position in Polar Space

In the polar coordinate system, the position vector $\vec{r}$ pointing from the origin directly to the particle's coordinate is defined simply as its magnitude $r$ in the radial unit direction $\hat{r}$.

$$\vec{r} = r \hat{r}$$
Step 2 Bridge To Cartesian Space

Decomposing Polar Unit Vectors

To compute the derivative of moving polar vectors, we map them onto stationary Cartesian unit vectors $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$:

$$\hat{r} = \cos\theta\hat{i} + \sin\theta\hat{j}$$
$$\hat{\theta} = -\sin\theta\hat{i} + \cos\theta\hat{j}$$
Step 3 Calculus Chain Rule

Differentiating the Unit Radial Vector

We apply the chain rule of differentiation. Note that as the angle $\theta(t)$ changes with time, we take the derivative of $\hat{r}$ with respect to $\theta$, then multiply by the rate of change $\frac{d\theta}{dt}$:

$$\frac{d\hat{r}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\cos\theta\hat{i} + \sin\theta\hat{j}\right) = \left(-\sin\theta\hat{i} + \cos\theta\hat{j}\right) \frac{d\theta}{dt}$$

Notice that the expression in brackets matches our definition of $\hat{\theta}$ exactly! Thus:

$$\frac{d\hat{r}}{dt} = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\hat{\theta}$$
Step 4 Velocity Result

The Final Velocity Vector Formulation

Substituting our computed derivative of $\hat{r}$ back into our velocity formula (since magnitude $r$ remains a constant in perfect circular motion):

$$\vec{v} = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = r \frac{d\hat{r}}{dt} = r \frac{d\theta}{dt} \hat{\theta} = r \omega \hat{\theta}$$
This proves that velocity is purely tangential in circular motion, and its magnitude scales directly with both radius and angular speed!

Why does $d\hat{r}$ point along $\hat{\theta}$?

Critical Question Sandbox

In the video transcript, the instructor states: "The magnitude r being a constant with time will come out of the derivative..." Let's think beyond the simplification.

If the radius changes over time (such as in a spiral or elliptical trajectory), we must apply the **product rule** of differentiation when taking the derivative of $\vec{r} = r \hat{r}$.

The full general derivative formula becomes:

$$\vec{v} = \frac{d}{dt}(r\\hat{r}) = \frac{dr}{dt}\hat{r} + r \frac{d\hat{r}}{dt} = \dot{r}\hat{r} + r\omega\hat{\theta}$$

This yields two distinct velocity components: a radial speed component ($\dot{r}$) along $\hat{r}$, plus the tangential speed component ($r \dot{\theta}$) along $\hat{\theta}$. Try simulating high speed or changing the coordinates to visualize this!

The unit tangential vector $\hat{\theta}$ is perpendicular to $\hat{r}$ in the counter-clockwise direction (increasing $\theta$).

To shift a vector counter-clockwise by $90^\circ$ ($\frac{\pi}{2}$ radians), we add $\frac{\pi}{2}$ to the angle arguments:

$$\hat{\theta} = \cos\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\hat{i} + \sin\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\hat{j}$$

Using trigonometric identities: $\cos(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}) = -\sin\theta$ and $\sin(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}) = \cos\theta$. This yields the exact formula!
