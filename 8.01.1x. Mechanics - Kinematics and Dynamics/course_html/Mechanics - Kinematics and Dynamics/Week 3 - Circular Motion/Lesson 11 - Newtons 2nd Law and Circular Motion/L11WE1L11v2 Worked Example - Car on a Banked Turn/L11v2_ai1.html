💡 The Physics Intuition & Master Mental Models

🔍 1. Why don't we resolve forces parallel to the incline? In basic inclined plane problems, the acceleration points down the slope. Hence, we rotate the axis. But on a banked circular turn, the acceleration is strictly **horizontal (radially inward)** towards the center of rotation. Resolving parallel to the incline would require decomposing both the acceleration and gravitational force. Selecting horizontal (r-hat) and vertical (k-hat) axes isolates acceleration to a single coordinate axis, dramatically simplifying calculations.
🔄 2. The Feedback Loop of Frictionless Incline Balance If there is no friction, the only force that can push the car inward is the horizontal component of the normal force, N * sin(φ). If the car travels too fast, the required centripetal force exceeds the horizontal normal component. The excess momentum forces the car to climb the hill, increasing its orbit radius. As it climbs, the current radius r increases, reducing the required centripetal acceleration (v^2/r) until it matches what the normal force can supply!
🧠 Critical Thinking Checkpoint: "What happens if φ is 90 degrees?" The track becomes a vertical wall. To stay pinned, you would need infinite centripetal force unless friction exists. In a frictionless universe, if φ is 0 (flat road), the ideal velocity is exactly 0. You cannot make a turn at any speed without slipping off!

🧮 Dynamic Step-by-Step Mathematical Engine

Step 1: Coordinate Frame & Balance Active

We break the Normal force N into horizontal and vertical components:

• Vertical Balance: N * cos(φ) - m * g = 0
→ N * cos(φ) = m * g
• Horizontal (Radial) Force: F_r = - N * sin(φ)
→ N * sin(φ) = m * v^2 / r
Step 2: Ideal Balanced Speed Equation Solved

Dividing the horizontal component by the vertical component eliminates mass m and normal force N:

• tan(φ) = [N * sin(φ)] / [N * cos(φ)] = [m * v^2 / r] / [m * g]
• tan(φ) = v^2 / (r * g)
• v_ideal = sqrt(r_0 * g * tan(φ))

Step 3: Off-Design Mechanics (v' or v'') Active Trajectory

If the driver sets an off-design velocity v_actual:

1. Target Radius for Static Equilibrium:
r_eq = v_actual^2 / (g * tan(φ)) = 31.2 m
2. Differential Equation of Motion on Slope:
m * s''(t) = F_net_parallel = m * (v_actual^2 * cos(φ) / r(t) - g * sin(φ))
Where s(t) represents the position coordinates along the incline. Because of mass inertia, the car overshoots the target radius r_eq and undergoes bounded oscillations!

💻 Computational Engine & Integration Algorithm

To compute the trajectory of the vehicle on the frictionless slope in real time, the simulation implements an Euler-Cromer numerical integrator. We model the mechanical system using a one-dimensional coordinate s representing distance along the slope face:

// 1. Determine local instantaneous radius
let r_current = r_base + s * cos(phi);
// 2. Compute net force along slope
let centrifugal_component = (v_actual^2 / r_current) * cos(phi);
let gravity_component = g * sin(phi);
let a_slope = centrifugal_component - gravity_component;
// 3. Update velocity and position
s_vel = s_vel + a_slope * dt;
s_pos = s_pos + s_vel * dt;

An artificial dampening factor of 0.05 is applied to model small air drag, allowing the car to eventually settle into its new stable circular path rather than oscillating infinitely.
