1. Polar Coordinate Shift

Instead of Cartesian coordinates \((x,y)\), circular motion is elegantly governed by Polar basis vectors: $$\hat{r} = \cos\theta \hat{i} + \sin\theta \hat{j}$$ $$\hat{\theta} = -\sin\theta \hat{i} + \cos\theta \hat{j}$$ The velocity vector \(\vec{v}\) points exclusively in the dynamic tangential \(\hat{\theta}\) direction.

2. Tangential Speed Constant

Under uniform circular motion, the angular sweep rate \(\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\) is strictly constant. Because position is \(\vec{r} = r\hat{r}\), taking the first derivative gives the tangential velocity vector: $$\vec{v} = r\omega\hat{\theta}$$ Its magnitude remains constant, but its direction alters continuously!

3. The Inward Vector Pull

Acceleration is the derivative of velocity: \(\vec{a} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}\). Since the direction of \(\vec{v}\) turns towards the center, the vector delta \(\Delta\vec{v}\) over any small time \(\Delta t\) pulls straight inward, causing the inward component: $$\vec{a}_r = -r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2 \hat{r}$$

The Elegant Mathematical Derivation

1. Let the position of a particle tracing a circle of radius \(r\) be written in terms of cartesian coordinates: $$\vec{r}(t) = r\cos\theta(t)\hat{i} + r\sin\theta(t)\hat{j}$$

2. To find the instantaneous velocity, we take the time derivative using the chain rule, recognizing that \(\theta(t)\) is a function of time: $$\vec{v}(t) = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = r\left(-\sin\theta\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)\hat{i} + r\left(\cos\theta\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)\hat{j}$$

Factoring out the constant rate of change \(\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\): $$\vec{v}(t) = r\omega \underbrace{\left(-\sin\theta\hat{i} + \cos\theta\hat{j}\right)}_{\hat{\theta}} = r\omega\hat{\theta}$$

3. Now, we compute the acceleration vector \(\vec{a}(t)\) by taking the derivative of velocity \(\vec{v}(t)\) with respect to time again: $$\vec{a}(t) = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left[r\omega(-\sin\theta\hat{i} + \cos\theta\hat{j})\right]$$ $$\vec{a}(t) = r\omega\left(-\cos\theta\frac{d\theta}{dt}\hat{i} - \sin\theta\frac{d\theta}{dt}\hat{j}\right)$$

Factoring out another factor of \(\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\): $$\vec{a}(t) = -r\omega^2 \underbrace{\left(\cos\theta\hat{i} + \sin\theta\hat{j}\right)}_{\hat{r}} = -r\omega^2\hat{r}$$

Thus, we prove the magnitude is \(a_r = r\omega^2\) and the direction is strictly in the $-\hat{r}$ (inward radial) direction.

Interactive Critical Thinking Challenges

Q: Since speed is constant, why is there acceleration?

Acceleration is a vector quantity defined as \(\vec{a} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}\). Since velocity has both magnitude and direction, any change in direction requires a non-zero acceleration vector. In this case, the direction changes, creating inward acceleration without altering speed.

Q: What happens if the cord snaps?

If the centripetal constraint is severed, the centripetal force immediately drops to zero (\(\vec{a} = 0\)). Following Newton's First Law (inertia), the particle will escape the circular orbit, travelling along a straight line tangent to the circle at the moment of release.
