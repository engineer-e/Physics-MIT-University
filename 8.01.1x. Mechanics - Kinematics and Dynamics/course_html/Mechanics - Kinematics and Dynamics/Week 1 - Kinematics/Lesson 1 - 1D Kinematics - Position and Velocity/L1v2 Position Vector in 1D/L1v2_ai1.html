How do we describe position mathematically?

To study motion, we establish an origin, model complex moving objects (like runners with swinging limbs) as a single fixed point, and define a dynamic position vector:

Position Vector: $\vec{r}(t) = x(t) \hat{i}$ Component: $x(t)$ Unit Vector: $\hat{i}$

Sign & Direction Rules

+
Runner is Right of Origin

Component $x(t) > 0$. The position vector points right: parallel to $\hat{i}$.

0
Runner is at Origin

Component $x(t) = 0$. The position vector is null: $\vec{r}(t) = \vec{0}$.

-
Runner is Left of Origin

Component $x(t) < 0$. The vector points left: antiparallel to $\hat{i}$.

The Non-Rigid Body Abstraction

A real runner stretches, bends, and swings limbs continuously. Simulating all of these movements complicates equations. We choose a fixed reference point—often close to the center of mass—to represent the entire athlete as a single point particle.

Why do this? This abstracts away rotational and interior kinematics, leaving clean, simple translational formulas.

Component vs. Vector Definition

Do not confuse the scalar component $x(t)$ with the position vector $\vec{r}(t)$. The component carries the absolute magnitude and algebraic sign ($+$, $-$, $0$), while the vector represents the physical arrow pointing in space.

Formula structure: Vector = Component (with sign) $\times$ Unit Vector direction, meaning $\vec{r}(t) = x(t)\hat{i}$.

The Power of Unit Vector $\hat{i}$

The unit vector $\hat{i}$ acts as our geometric anchor. Because we're in one dimension, it points straight to the positive x-direction. When multiplied by a negative component, the negative sign algebraically turns the direction around, pointing the vector backwards.

Magnitude rule: A unit vector's magnitude is exactly $|\hat{i}| = 1$, meaning it never scales the original length value.
