Core Physics Terms

A vector has both magnitude and direction. In 1D kinematics along a single horizontal axis, directions are encoded simply by positive ($+$) or negative ($-$) signs multiplying the unit vector $\hat{i}$.

  • Position vector $\vec{r}(t)$: Locates the runner relative to the origin at timestamp $t$. Points from $0 \rightarrow x(t)$.
  • Position vector $\vec{r}(t+\Delta t)$: Locates the runner at later timestamp $t+\Delta t$. Points from $0 \rightarrow x(t+\Delta t)$.
  • Displacement vector $\Delta\vec{r}$: The straight-line vector change over interval $\Delta t$. Points from $x(t) \rightarrow x(t+\Delta t)$.
Sign Convention: While the distance traveled is always positive, the displacement component $\Delta x$ carries the sign pointing either rightward (positive) or leftward (negative).

Scalar Quantity

Distance

Definition: Is the length of the path travelled by an object between two points in space. From its definition, the distance is a scalar and it is always a positive quantity.

$\text{Distance} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |v(t)| dt \ge 0$
Vector Quantity

Displacement

Definition: Is the change in the position of an object. If at time $t = t_1$ the object is at position $\vec{r}(t_1)$, and at a later time $t = t_2 > t_1$ the object is at position $\vec{r}(t_2)$, the displacement vector is defined as: $$\Delta \vec{r} = \vec{r}(t_2) - \vec{r}(t_1)$$

In one dimension, the displacement vector has one component. For example, if the motion is along the x-axis, the displacement vector becomes: $$\Delta \vec{r} = \Delta x \hat{i} = (x(t_2) - x(t_1)) \hat{i}$$

  • Positive: when final position is larger than initial position.
  • Negative: when final position is smaller than initial position.
  • Zero: if the object ends at the starting point.

Displacement Scenario Breakdown

According to physics, displacement components carry physical meaning and can represent three primary physical outcomes over any given time interval $t_1 \rightarrow t_2$. Let's analyze them visually:

+

Positive Component ($\Delta x > 0$)

The runner ends up further to the right relative to where they started at time $t_1$. The displacement vector points in the positive $+x$ direction.

$x(t_2) > x(t_1)$
Vector points RIGHT
0

Zero Displacement ($\Delta x = 0$)

The person could run forward, loop around, and at time $t_2$, return to the exact same coordinates. Net displacement is zero, even though path distance is not.

$x(t_2) == x(t_1)$
Displacement is ZERO
-

Negative Component ($\Delta x < 0$)

At the end of the interval, the runner is located to the left of their original starting point. The displacement vector points in the negative $-x$ direction.

$x(t_2) < x(t_1)$
Vector points LEFT
Distance Traveled vs. Displacement

Notice how displacement only describes the net difference in coordinate values between the initial and final timestamps. If a runner covers a total of 100 meters on a running track and ends up right where they started, their distance traveled is 100 meters, but their displacement vector is zero. This is the exact principle described in the lecture transcript.
